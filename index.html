Composition & Code

An Open Educational Resource for College Writing

This is a freely available assignment designed for college-level English and composition courses. It was built to be used, adapted, and remixed. Instructors are encouraged to modify it for their course context.

This resource is licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 (CC BY 4.0), which permits others to adapt, tweak, and build upon this work, even for commercial purposes, provided they credit the original source.

About this assignment

Composition & Code asks students to compose a piece of writing — a personal narrative, a portfolio, or a creative piece — and publish it as a web page using HTML and CSS. Students move through four phases: Choose, Plan, Write, Build.

1

Brainstorm

Freewrite, surface your ideas, and choose a direction.

2

Plan

Create a blueprint for your website.

3

Write

Write the content for the section(s) of your planned website.

4

Build

Bring your writing to life with code.

Learning Objectives & Goals

By the end of this assignment, you will be able to:

  • Generate and develop an original idea through structured freewriting and reflection
  • Write intentionally for a specific audience and purpose
  • Make deliberate compositional choices about voice, structure, and detail
  • Translate a written draft into a structured, readable web page
  • Make intentional design decisions about typography, color, and spacing that serve your content
  • Use HTML to structure content semantically
  • Use CSS to style and present content visually
  • Reflect on your own creative and technical process across all four phases

Why should composition include code?

Writing has never existed apart from its medium. The clay tablet, the printed page, the word processor. Each technology has reshaped not just how writing is delivered, but how it is composed, revised, and read. The web is no different. Writing instruction that incorporates code asks students to engage with language simultaneously on rhetorical and structural levels. As Dan Martin suggests, "teaching students how to use HTML and CSS without these elements eliminates an important linguistic and cognitive connection between the language and the experiences that ultimately shape how it is used to solve real problems" (2026). When markup is taught in isolation from writing, students learn syntax without purpose. When it is taught alongside composition, it is embedded in the act of making meaning for a real audience and thus, code becomes a rhetorical tool rather than a technical hurdle.

Research shows that digital multimodal composition encourages students to represent themselves in empowering ways and that giving students agency over how they communicate. This assignment legitimizes personal narrative, creative writing, and professional self-presentation alongside the traditional essay, making it a meaningful act of inclusion. Multimodal composition assignments have also been shown to increase student motivation and deepen engagement with the writing process. When the output is something a student can share, link to, and own beyond the course, the stakes of revision feel different. Writing for an actual audience, even a small one, changes how students think about voice, structure, and purpose.

Explore the Assignment

The final deliverable can be one of the following project types:

Project Type Description
Personal Narrative A true story from your life
Short Story A fictional narrative with a character(s)
Professional Portfolio A curated self-presentation that includes a bio and displays your work and projects
Digital Essay An argument or topic on something you are interested in

Not sure what project you would like to build yet? Phase 1: Brainstorm might help you gain some clarity.

Phase 1

Brainstorm

Freewrite. Surface your ideas. Follow the thought.

1

Freewrite

Set a timer for 10 minutes — pen and paper recommended. Start from your own ideas or pick a prompt:

"A story I've never written down but think about often is…"
"If someone found a page I made, I'd want them to know…"
"I've always wanted to write about _____ but didn't think it counted."
"A moment that changed how I see myself or the world was…"
2

Reflect

  • What surprised you in what you wrote?
  • Is there a project type that fits what came up?
  • Who is your audience and what do you want them to feel or know?
3

Write a project proposal

One paragraph. Include: project type · working title · audience · why it matters.

Example

"I am creating a personal narrative site titled 'Stuck Inside' about my experience during the COVID-19 quarantine. My audience is anyone who lived through that period of isolation and found unexpected ways to cope. This project matters to me because I have never written about that time, and I think putting it into words and into a page I can share is a way of finally processing it and sharing it with people who might have had similar experiences."

Phase 1 Deliverables

Your freewriting (for your eyes only) Project proposal

Phase 2

Plan

Wireframe your site.

A wireframe is a blueprint. It maps out what goes where on your site before you write a line of code or a line of prose. It doesn't have to be beautiful!

1

Decide your structure

Think through these questions before you start sketching:

  • How many pages or sections will your site have?
  • What does a visitor see first?
  • Where does the main writing live?
  • Will you include images? A header? A navigation menu?
2

Sketch your wireframe

You can wireframe on paper or digitally.

  • Paper: Sketch each page or section as a simple box diagram. Label what goes where.
  • Digital: Use Excalidraw (free, no account needed), Figma (free tier), or Google Slides.

Your wireframe should show at minimum: the layout of your main page, where your writing will appear, and any navigation or structural elements.

3

Annotate

Add brief notes to your wireframe about content and tone. What is the mood of this page? What do you want the reader to feel in this section?

Phase 2 Deliverables

Wireframe (sketch or digital file)

Phase 3

Write

Draft your written content.

With your wireframe in hand, write the actual content for your site in a document, not a code editor – we'll get to that soon.

1

Draft section by section

Use your wireframe as a guide. Write the content for each section of your site in a Google Doc or Word document. Draft your headings, body text, and any captions or labels.

2

Meet the writing requirements

All projects must include:

  • A minimum of 600–800 words of original prose
  • A clear, consistent voice and sense of audience
  • A title and any section headings you plan to use on the site
3

Peer review

Exchange your draft with a classmate for feedback before moving to the build phase.

Phase 3 Deliverables

Full written content draft

Phase 4

Build

Bring your writing to life.

Your writing is done. Your wireframe is mapped out. Now you build the home for your writing.

1

Set up your workspace

Download the starter template from the Resources page. Open it in VS Code. The template includes a basic index.html and style.css with comments to guide you.

2

Pour in your content

Copy your writing from your draft document into the HTML file. Use the tags and structure from the starter template. Your wireframe tells you where everything goes.

3

Style your page

Open your style.css and make intentional design choices:

  • Choose a font from Google Fonts that fits the tone of your writing
  • Choose a color palette — background, text, accents
  • Adjust spacing so your writing is easy to read

Minimum requirements: one index.html with semantic structure + one linked external style.css.

4

Publish

Publish your site using GitHub Pages and submit the link to your instructor.

5

Write your reflection

In 200–300 words, respond to the following:

  • What did you learn about your own writing by designing around it?
  • What was harder — the writing or the building?
  • What would you do differently with more time?
  • Who have you shared this with, or who do you want to share it with?

Phase 4 Deliverables

Published site link Reflection
